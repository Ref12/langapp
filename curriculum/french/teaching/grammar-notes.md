# French grammar: constructions, senses, and forms

Grammar is an independently addressable learning inventory, not a way to
award vocabulary credit for a vaguely related dictionary meaning. A learner
can use a fixed construction appropriately without demonstrating every
component as an independently retrievable lexical item.

The explanations, placements, and examples in this teaching program are
original AI-assisted material requiring human linguistic and pedagogical
review. They are not taken from a paid course, textbook, or examination.
Source-backed lexical or morphological information retains its own provenance.

## What the progression covers

The first phase introduces articles and gender with useful nouns, agreement
in short descriptions, personal reference, high-use present-tense verbs,
basic questions and negatives, and polite requests. Teach `tu` and `vous`
as choices involving relationship, setting, and number, not an automatic
age-based rule. The first four levels form a practical interaction route,
not exhaustive mastery of the present tense.

Everyday-life work expands adjective and possessive agreement, partitives,
quantity expressions, negative quantities, pronominal verbs, imperatives,
completed events, and future plans. Revisit `être` and `avoir` as auxiliaries
under the relevant constructions rather than assuming a lexical identity
exercise established compound-tense use.

Connected narration distinguishes `passé composé` events from `imparfait`
background or habitual circumstances. Their choice depends on the intended
view of an event, not simply its duration. Instructions and message work
develop object reference, clitic placement, relative clauses, and indirect
questions. Comparison and conditional requests support practical decisions.

Problem-solving work develops `y` and `en`, fuller clitic ordering, passive
and impersonal alternatives, purpose, cause, concession, future/conditional
contrasts, and common subjunctive constructions. Teach indicative/subjunctive
contrasts through meaning and construction, not a claim that one mood always
means fact and the other always means uncertainty.

Analysis and argument revisit reported speech, tense relationships,
counterfactuals, qualification, nominalization, reference, and scope. Later
work adds subtle stance and register contrasts, idiomatic restrictions,
information structure, and recognition of marked narrative forms.
Recognition of `passé simple` in reading does not make its conversational
production a general core requirement. Rarer literary constructions belong
in the optional literary route when their purpose is contextual reading.

## Forms are not extra headwords

Conjugation, gender, number, elision, and spelling variants need explicit
relationships to lexical identities. A verb's infinitive and its conjugated
forms do not become separate lemmas merely because their spellings differ.
An additional genuinely distinct sense can be taught without increasing
lemma breadth.

Preserve accents and distinguish lexical homographs. `Fils` as a son and
`fils` as the plural of a thread do not have the same lexical identity or
pronunciation. Similarly, `est` in a compass description and a form of
`être` cannot be matched by spelling alone.

An article or pronoun may have inflected or elided forms while a similarly
written form has another grammatical function. The fixed forms in
`est-ce que`, `il y a`, or a polite request can be taught through a
construction without inventing a dictionary sense or asserting that every
component is independently understood.

## Prerequisites and examples

Lexical anchors name precise senses necessary for a construction. They are
not an exhaustive segmentation of its written template. Construction
prerequisites separately identify earlier or explicitly co-taught grammar,
such as an auxiliary pattern or a clitic-placement rule.

Examples must use already available material or identify their extra
support. An unfamiliar example word is not silently a new required lemma.
English support should clarify the relevant sense and contrast without
giving away the answer in a recognition task.

Tourist phrases use explicit surface realizations linked to the taught
lexical or construction IDs. Elided and contracted text, verb endings,
agreement, and fixed expressions must be covered. Merely finding all the
component IDs somewhere in the dictionary does not establish that the
phrase's surface is explained.

These links are structural checks. They do not certify the idiomaticity of
an authored sentence, the naturalness of its connected-speech transcription,
or the learner's ability to choose it in a real interaction.

## Register, variation, and evidence

Distinguish conventional written negation from common conversational
omission of `ne`. Recognition of a conversational reduction is not a rule
to remove it in every setting. Present intonation questions, `est-ce que`,
and inversion as context-sensitive alternatives rather than universal
substitutions.

Pronunciation, liaison, silent letters, and nasal-vowel contrasts need their
own explanation; see `pronunciation.md`. Text-only work does not establish
listening or pronunciation. Keep evidence about the precise sense,
construction, phrase, modality, and assistance as defined in `mastery.yaml`.
