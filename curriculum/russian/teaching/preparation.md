# Before level 1: Cyrillic and a usable reading routine

This English-supported orientation is preparation, not another numbered
level or extra vocabulary breadth. Work in short sessions. The goal is to
recognize and enter Russian text, notice where a reading is uncertain, and
know which questions require actual audio or human help.

## 1. Meet the printed and typed alphabet

Russian uses these 33 letters, shown in uppercase and lowercase:

```text
А а  Б б  В в  Г г  Д д  Е е  Ё ё  Ж ж  З з  И и  Й й
К к  Л л  М м  Н н  О о  П п  Р р  С с  Т т  У у  Ф ф
Х х  Ц ц  Ч ч  Ш ш  Щ щ  Ъ ъ  Ы ы  Ь ь  Э э  Ю ю  Я я
```

Practice matching a lowercase letter to its uppercase form, then finding
the same letter in a short label. Use clearly printed or typed examples
first; a font change is a new recognition condition, not a new word.
Alphabet familiarity is not a claim to know a word's meaning or stress.

### Watch the deceptive Latin lookalikes

| Russian letter | Trap for an English reader |
| --- | --- |
| `В в` | Its shape is not the English letter B; it commonly represents a v-like consonant. |
| `Н н` | It is not English H; it represents an n-like consonant. |
| `Р р` | It is not English P; its Russian consonant needs audio and is not ordinary English r. |
| `С с` | Do not alternate English hard and soft C readings; Russian uses an s-like consonant here. |
| `У у` | It is not the English letter Y; learn the Russian vowel with audio. |
| `Х х` | It is not the English sequence usually represented by x; learn its fricative with audio. |
| `Е е` | Do not read it as an English letter name; its effect depends partly on context. |

These descriptions orient recognition; they are not phonetic transcriptions
or sufficient pronunciation instructions. `А`, `К`, `М`, `О`, and `Т` may
look reassuringly familiar but still require Russian sound practice.
Lowercase `а`, `е`, `о`, `р`, `с`, and `х` can also be typed accidentally
with Latin code points that only look right.

**Text task:** select the Cyrillic `р` rather than Latin `p`, and explain
which is valid in a Russian word. The result is reading/script discrimination,
not spoken-production evidence.

## 2. Treat stress as information, not decoration

Russian lexical stress is not safely predictable from a word's ending.
It can move between inflected forms; an unstressed spelling alone may not
distinguish homographs. Learn the intended sense and the approved reading
together rather than transferring stress from a different sense or form.

In compact entries, `ch` is ordinary Cyrillic spelling. `pr` is a Cyrillic
lexical-stress aid derived from documented source markings. A combining
acute follows the stressed vowel: `а́` is an **illustration of the notation**,
not a proposed reading of a vocabulary entry. Source apostrophes become
these acute marks mechanically. Monosyllables and explicitly established
unstressed function words need not receive a mark.

If the source lacks reliable multisyllabic stress, contains conflicting
marks, or disagrees on `е` and `ё`, the adapter reports a gap. The tutor must
not guess a missing reading, derive it from English, or assume the stress
of an unrelated inflection. Grammar can remain teachable as a fixed form
while its source-backed compact phrase reading awaits resolution.

The dictionary-based aid does **not** encode actual phrase prominence,
vowel reduction, consonant assimilation, palatalization detail, intonation,
or an observed speaker's performance. A phrase assembled from approved
component readings is an authored assembly, not a source recording.

## 3. Keep `е` and `ё` distinct

`Ё ё` is part of the alphabet, not an optional accent invented by this
course. Ordinary publications sometimes print `е` where careful spelling
would have `ё`; this curriculum preserves documented meaningful distinctions.
For example, the spellings `все` and `всё` must not be blindly normalized
into one lexical reading or one sense.

In many familiar words `ё` makes the stressed vowel apparent, but it is not
a universal algorithm for compound stress or all exceptional words. Nor
does a source `ё` prove which homograph sense was intended. Do not convert
every source `е` to `ё` from a model's confidence. Likewise, keep `е` and `э`
distinct instead of treating them as two fonts for one vowel.

**Text task:** preserve `ё` when copying an approved entry and compare it
with an `е` spelling without awarding extra headword credit. Explain that
the learner has shown orthographic attention, not accurate vowel production.

## 4. Hard and soft consonants

Russian distinguishes many hard and soft consonant pairs. Softness involves
the tongue position during the consonant, not merely adding a separate
English y sound after every letter. The following vowel letter, a soft sign,
and the identity of the consonant can all matter.

- `е`, `ё`, `и`, `ю`, and `я` commonly indicate a preceding soft paired
  consonant in ordinary native vocabulary, but spelling alone is not a
  universal rule for every consonant or loanword.
- Word-initially and after vowels or separating signs, some of those
  letters have a different sound sequence from their use after a consonant.
- `ь` has no independent vowel sound. It can mark softness, a grammatical
  spelling, or separation depending on context.
- `ъ` has no independent vowel sound either. In modern ordinary spelling,
  it often separates a prefix from a following iotated-vowel letter.
- `ж`, `ш`, and `ц` are normally hard, while `ч` and `щ` are normally soft
  in the introductory standard patterns. Learn real exceptions with
  verified examples, not by extending a slogan to all speech.
- `й` is a consonant letter; `ы` is a vowel requiring focused audio practice.

Use a qualified speaker or reliable audio for perceptual contrasts and
feedback. A tutor can explain the contrast in text but cannot honestly
score the learner's articulation from a typed answer.

## 5. Reduction, assimilation, and connected speech

A stressed vowel and an unstressed vowel with the same spelling may sound
different. Unstressed `о` and `а`, and unstressed front vowels in different
consonant contexts, require listening practice rather than letter-by-letter
English readings. Consonants may change voicing near other consonants or
at word edges. A word spoken alone does not supply every connected-speech
realization.

Do not respell `ch` to imitate reduction, remove meaningful letters because
they are not separately audible, or pretend that acute marks are an IPA
transcription. The consistent `pr` field is a lexical aid only. Separate
questions about dictionary stress, phrase focus, and actual pronunciation.

**Audio task, only when audio exists:** hear two realizations and identify
the intended approved item, then record the listening conditions and help
received. Reading the answer or seeing a stress mark first may reveal the
answer; record that assistance rather than calling it unaided listening.

## 6. Enter and inspect Cyrillic

1. Enable a Russian keyboard layout using your device's language/input
   settings. Menu names and shortcuts vary; use the operating system's
   documentation rather than assuming one universal shortcut.
2. Use an on-screen keyboard to locate unfamiliar keys. Learn where that
   layout puts `ё`, `ь`, `ъ`, and Russian punctuation.
3. Type a short approved entry in Cyrillic, compare it with the reference,
   and check for mixed Latin/Cyrillic characters. Do not silently replace
   `ё` with `е` because its key is inconvenient.
4. Copy approved `pr` marks if needed for study. Learners need not produce
   combining acute marks as ordinary Russian spelling, and the marks must
   not be added from a guessed stress pattern.
5. If using transliteration input, confirm the actual resulting Cyrillic
   spelling. The input method is assistance; its suggestions do not
   establish independent spelling or lexical recall.

Expose learners separately to italics and handwriting: Russian italic or
cursive letterforms can look unlike print, including some forms of `т`,
`д`, and `г`. Recognizing a handwritten sample does not demonstrate writing
one. Do not assign a handwriting stage without an actual handwritten sample.

## Ready to start, not “alphabet mastered”

A useful readiness check asks the learner to:

- Match common printed letter forms and notice a misleading Latin lookalike.
- Locate the stress mark in an approved `pr` and explain when stress is unknown.
- Preserve `е`/`ё` in a short Cyrillic copying or typing task.
- Enter a short label and ask for repetition or a written version when needed.

Record what was actually observed using `mastery.yaml`; untested listening,
speech, and handwriting remain unassessed. Begin level 1 while fragile
letter and input skills continue in short review.
